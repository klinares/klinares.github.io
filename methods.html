Methodological Foundation

Total Survey Error and the Total Error Framework as a working reference

The portfolio cards under "Projects" show what I do. This page shows the framework I do it within. My methodological training centers on the Total Survey Error (TSE) framework developed by Groves and colleagues, extended to Big Data through the Total Error Framework (TEF) of Amaya, Biemer, and Kinyon (2020). Both frameworks decompose data quality into the same eight error components, which keeps reasoning about quality consistent whether the data come from a probability survey, a web scrape, an administrative record system, or an AI-mediated instrument.

Eight components, two frameworks, one shared diagnostic vocabulary.

The Total Survey Error (TSE) framework, developed across the survey methodology canon (Groves, 2004; Groves et al., 2004; Biemer, 2010; Groves and Lyberg, 2010), enumerates the distinct sources of error that can affect survey estimates and treats them collectively as the basis for evaluating data quality. Understanding the contribution of each component lets the analyst adjust survey design, processing steps, and analytic methods to minimize or compensate for them.

Amaya, Biemer, and Kinyon (2020) extended this thinking through the Total Error Framework (TEF). Big Data, including web-scraped content, social media, administrative records, transactional logs, and other digital trace sources, increasingly supplements or replaces survey data, but it carries its own error structure. The TEF retains the same eight error components as TSE while recognizing that the Big Data workflow differs: it begins by identifying preexisting data sources rather than fielding a probability sample, and it Extracts, Transforms, and Loads the data rather than collecting it directly.

What makes the TEF powerful is that it gives both data types a shared diagnostic vocabulary. The methodologist can compare error sources across data types, reason about how survey and Big Data sources combine in mixed designs, and decide on principled grounds when each source is preferable for a given research question.

The eight error components

01

Coverage / domain error

Mismatch between the units represented in the data and those in the target population, including undercoverage, overcoverage, and duplication.

In surveysFrame undercoverage of cell-phone-only households; out-of-scope businesses on a residential frame; multiple landlines linked to the same household.

In Big DataTwitter users skew younger than the general population; corporate accounts appear in user-focused analyses; people maintain multiple accounts that cannot be deduplicated.

02

Sampling error

Imprecision arising from analyzing a subset of the population rather than the full population.

In surveysDriven by sample size, design effects from stratification and clustering, and weight variation under unequal probability designs.

In Big DataOften minimal because the full data set may be analyzable as a census; in massive samples, however, even practically meaningless differences become statistically significant.

03

Specification error

Mismatch between the concept the research question requires and the concept actually captured in the data.

In surveysConstructs that are difficult to operationalize unambiguously, such as housing-unit square footage, social engagement, or self-rated health.

In Big DataConcepts inconsistent with the available data because variables were defined at the source, with little researcher control over how they were measured or recorded.

04

Nonresponse / missing data error

Bias and imprecision introduced when records lack values on key variables.

In surveysUnit nonresponse when a sampled case refuses or is unreachable, and item nonresponse when a respondent skips a specific question.

In Big DataItem and unit missingness, often confounded with undercoverage because the cause of missingness is rarely observable.

05

Measurement / content error

Erroneous values on variables that are present in the data, both unintentional and deliberate.

In surveysMemory error, comprehension errors, social-desirability bias, interviewer effects, and deliberate misreporting on sensitive topics.

In Big DataMechanical and transcription errors, outdated entries, false readings from devices, and self-presentation effects on social platforms.

06

Processing error

Errors introduced after data collection by editing, transformation, and linkage.

In surveysEditing errors and keying errors during data entry, coding, and cleaning.

In Big DataEditing errors plus data linkage errors when joining records across sources without a common, clean identifier.

07

Modeling / estimation error

Errors arising from analytic adjustments such as imputation, calibration, weighting, and missing-data corrections.

In surveysImputation, calibration, weighting, and nonresponse adjustments are familiar tools with well-studied properties.

In Big DataThe same tools apply, but the missing-data mechanism is often unknown and harder to model than in surveys, and external calibration data may be needed.

08

Analytic error

Errors in interpreting results and drawing inference from the data.

In surveysInferences to small areas or rare subgroups are often difficult; biases and measurement variances are sometimes ignored in downstream analyses.

In Big DataHigh granularity supports small-area inference, but biases and measurement variances are often ignored, and the data are frequently univariate, limiting causal and quasi-experimental modeling.
